Chapters 1 and 3

Adding material instead of cutting it away

Additive manufacturing (AM), commonly called 3D printing, builds an object layer by layer from a digital model. Subtractive manufacturing starts with a block and removes material by cutting, milling, turning or drilling. The report compares the two across seven points.

PointAdditiveSubtractive
ProcessBuilds a part layer by layer from plastics, metals or compositesRemoves material from a block or sheet
Design freedomComplex geometry, internal structures, organic shapesLimited by tools and machining; suits simpler shapes
Material useAdds material only where neededSignificant waste, especially on intricate parts
Prototype or productionBegan with prototyping, now used for end-use partsHistorically the route for mass production
Tooling and setupMinimal; suits small batches and custom workNeeds cutting tools, fixtures and molds
WasteExcess powder and supports can often be recycledChips and shavings, not always recyclable
Part complexityLattices, internal features, lightweight designsSimpler, traditional shapes
Chapter 2 · Literature survey

What five recent papers found

Careri et al. · 2023

Heat exchangers for aerospace

Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) suits heat exchangers because it handles complex geometry and supports topology optimisation and CFD modelling. Leak-proof thin walls and the effect of process settings on surface quality remain hard, and high-strength aluminium alloys need more research.

Gonçalves et al. · 2023

Environmental and economic impact of metal AM

In the industrial machinery case, AM raised costs but gave large environmental benefits. In the aeronautical case it cut both cost and environmental impact, mainly through lower mass and part consolidation. Equipment investment, material choice and recycling strategy decide the outcome.

Paggi et al. · 2022

Contactless supports in metal printing

Contactless supports gave a more uniform surface, shorter printing time and a 66% drop in post-processing time. They worked on existing printers without hurting part properties. Problems appeared at angles above 10°.

Colosimo et al. · 2023

Thermal profiles in extrusion AM

A method that captures both timing and location of heat in extrusion printing, tested in simulation and on a Big Area Additive Manufacturing case. Local Moran's indexes pick out areas of high spatial correlation, and the authors point toward in-line process monitoring.

Lakshmanan et al. · 2023

Lean management meets AM

Digital tools for predictive AM cut waste and losses. Digital inventory, local manufacturing and streamlined processes improve cost and time. The paper stresses simulation-driven design for additive manufacturing (DfAM).

Chapters 4 and 6

Three ways to print

FDM

Fused deposition modeling

A spool of thermoplastic such as ABS or PLA is melted in a heated nozzle and extruded layer by layer onto a build platform. Simple, low cost and available in many materials. Used for prototypes and functional parts.

SLA

Stereolithography

A UV laser cures liquid photopolymer resin in a vat. The platform lifts out of the resin as each layer hardens. Gives high resolution and smooth surfaces for product design, jewellery and dentistry.

SLS

Selective laser sintering

A laser fuses powder, usually polymer or metal, one cross section at a time. The loose powder holds the part, so no support structures are needed. The rest of this page follows this process.

Rapid prototyping is the main use that made AM popular: a digital model is sliced into thin layers, built, tested and revised quickly, without molds or custom tooling. Material choice and post-processing such as curing or polishing still need planning.

Chapters 7 to 10 · Deep dive

Inside a selective laser sintering printer

SLS heats powder just below its melting point so the grains fuse. A laser, lenses and an X-Y scanning mirror trace each layer, a leveling roller spreads fresh powder, and pistons lower the build chamber after every pass.

  1. Prepare the bedA thin, even layer of powder is spread across the platform.
  2. SinterThe laser scans the cross section and partly melts the powder.
  3. Build upThe platform drops and fresh powder is spread over the last layer.
  4. Self-supportUnsintered powder holds overhangs, so no extra supports.
  5. CoolHeat from the laser fuses and solidifies the layer into a cohesive part.
  6. FinishThe part cools in the bed, excess powder is removed, then sanding or polishing.
Polymers

Nylon

Strong, durable and flexible. Used for functional prototypes and end-use parts, from automotive components to consumer goods.

Metals

Aluminium and steel

Lightweight, strong aluminium suits aerospace and automotive. Steel gives robust, durable parts for engineering use.

Ceramics

Zirconia and alumina

For high temperature resistance, electrical insulation or wear resistance, as in electronics and medical devices.

Chapter 5

Software that turns an idea into a print

Every print starts as a 3D model. The report surveys six modelling tools and says the choice depends on design complexity, the user's skill and the application.

AutoCAD

Precise 3D models and many file formats for AM. A staple in engineering and architecture for decades.

SolidWorks

Parametric modelling makes designs easy to change and iterate. Built-in simulation tests how a part behaves before it is printed.

Fusion 360

Cloud CAD/CAM from Autodesk with parametric modelling, generative design and shared work. Good for organic shapes and optimised structures.

TinkerCAD

Web-based and beginner friendly. Basic models and quick prototypes, and a gentle entry point to 3D modelling.

Blender

Open-source suite for modelling, animation and rendering. Suited to detailed, good-looking models, and to projects that also need animation.

OpenSCAD

Models are written as scripts instead of drawn. Favoured by programmers and engineers for parametric parts.

Chapter 12

What it gives and what it costs

Advantages

  • Design flexibility. Shapes that are hard or impossible with traditional methods.
  • Rapid prototyping. Quick, low-cost iteration before mass production.
  • Customisation. Each item in a run can be unique at little extra cost.
  • Less waste. Material is added layer by layer, not cut away.
  • One-piece assemblies. Fewer parts to join, often stronger and more durable.

Limits

  • Materials. Fewer options than traditional manufacturing for some uses.
  • Post-processing. Sanding, polishing or surface treatment adds time and cost.
  • Build speed. Slow for large or intricate parts and high volumes.
  • Equipment cost. Industrial machines are a barrier for small firms.
  • Surface finish. Layer lines and rough texture in some processes.
  • Material properties. Some traditional processes still give better strength or heat resistance.
Chapter 13

Conclusion

Additive manufacturing lets industry build complex geometries, cut material waste and prototype quickly, which makes it a driver of innovation. Material limits and post-processing remain, and research is steadily working on them. Getting the full benefit depends on collaboration between industry, continued research and a willingness to work through these challenges.
